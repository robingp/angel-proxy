What “easy to spell and remember” actually requires

These are the six tests I scored every remaining name against. The first four are about spelling; the last two are about memory.

TEST 1
Sounds like it looks
You can write it correctly after hearing it once, with no guessing.
TEST 2
No trap letters
No silent letters, no double letters, no “c or k or s” confusion.
TEST 3
No sound-alike words
Nothing that could be confused with another word that sounds identical.
TEST 4
Survives a phone call
You never have to say “no, with two O’s” to a customer.
TEST 5
Means something
Meaning is what memory holds on to. Random letters are forgotten in a day.
TEST 6
You can picture it
A concrete image sticks. An abstract idea does not.

This test changed my earlier advice

Two names I recommended last time fail Test 3, and I should withdraw them. Keelvane and Cairnvane both end in “vane” — which sounds exactly like vain and vein. Every customer who hears the name on a call has a one-in-three chance of typing the wrong website. That is a permanent tax on every conversation you will ever have. Good names, wrong ending.

Say them out loud, in this order

This is the test I cannot run for you, and it is the one that decides. Read each aloud, then imagine saying it to a customer on a phone call with a bad line:

  • “Hi, it’s Guru from Moorkeep.” — two beats, lands cleanly, sounds like an established company.
  • “Hi, it’s Guru from Keelsure.” — equally clean, slightly more corporate and cooler in tone.
  • “Hi, it’s Guru from Vouchkeep.” — strongest promise, very slightly more of a mouthful.

If one of them makes you hesitate, that is your answer — cross it out. If none of them do, take Moorkeep: it is the only one that scored full marks.
