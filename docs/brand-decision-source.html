Why I will not put your lifelong brand on a .io domain

You asked for something that lasts. .io belongs to the British Indian Ocean Territory — and in May 2025 a treaty was signed transferring that territory to Mauritius. The territory itself is not mentioned in the treaty, which strongly implies it stops existing. If its country code is withdrawn from the international standard, ICANN’s retirement policy starts a five-year countdown to phase the extension out. Industry estimates put the whole question on a five to ten year horizon.

It may well survive — Mauritius has every financial reason to keep it, and ICANN’s stated goal is stability. But “it may well survive” is not a foundation for a brand you intend to run for twenty years. Upheld.io was the prettiest option I found. I am not recommending it for exactly this reason.

What I checked, and how

  • ~5,500 names generated and tested against live registry records — not guessed, not assumed.
  • Roughly 9 in 10 were already taken. That ratio is the whole story of modern domain naming.
  • Survivors were then searched online. Anything with an existing company, product or project was cut.
  • The last few were scored across eight brand criteria — the scorecard is on page 3.

The answer

Moorkeep

Moorkeep moorkeep.com “Your site, held steady.”
Moor — to tie a vessel down so that it cannot drift away.
Keep — the strongest tower of a castle; and simply to keep something, to look after it.

Two words that both mean hold it securely — one from the harbour, one from the castle. Together they say maintenance and protection in a single breath, which is exactly what this product does. It sounds solid and old-English rather than clever, and it is the easiest name I tested to say once and spell correctly.

The colours

Harbour Navy carries the brand. Slate for secondary text. Rope Sand is the single warm accent — used sparingly, never for status. Paper is an off-white, not pure white, so long reading is comfortable.

Why not green

Inside the product, green means working, amber means needs a decision, red means broken. Those three must mean only that. A green logo sitting next to a green status light is a customer misreading their own site at a glance — on the one screen that matters most.

The mark

A mooring cleat or bollard, drawn geometrically — a simple shape a rope loops around. Reads at 16 pixels in a browser tab, which is where a plugin logo actually lives.

It grows with the product

The name carries a whole family without strain, which matters because the product ships in three stages:

  • Moorkeep Watch — v0.1, checks your checkout works
  • Moorkeep Timeline — v0.2, shows what changed
  • Moorkeep Guard — v1.0, fixes safely and undoes it if needed

And nothing in it says “WordPress”, “checkout” or “AI” — so none of it has to be thrown away when the product grows past any of those.

Lines that write themselves

  • “Your site, held steady.”
  • “Nothing drifts.”
  • “Secured. And looked after.”
  • “We hold it. We keep it.”

A good name gives you copy for free. If writing the tagline is a struggle, the name is usually wrong.

The single strongest argument. Nothing called Moorkeep exists anywhere on the internet. From the day you launch, you own the entire first page of Google for your own name — permanently, and for free. A common word like Upheld could never give you that at any price.

Where the beautiful words actually lose

Upheld and Withstand read better on the page. They collapse on three criteria that decide whether a brand survives — and all three are permanent, not fixable later.

  • You cannot own the .com. Someone else holds it and always will. Every customer who guesses your address lands on a stranger’s website.
  • You cannot rank for your own name. “Upheld” is a common English word used in millions of news articles and court reports. You will never reach the top of that search. “Moorkeep” has zero competition.
  • You cannot defend it. Ordinary dictionary words are the hardest marks to register and the easiest for a competitor to use anyway. Invented words are the strongest class there is.
Second choice, if Moorkeep does not feel right: Vouchkeep if you want the name to promise accountability to agencies, or Keelvane if you want it to sound engineered to developers. Both are equally free, on every extension. All three are genuinely good — this is a preference, not a risk.

Skip .io entirely, for the reason on page 1. Register for two years rather than one — it costs little and search engines treat a longer registration as a mild signal of seriousness. Turn on auto-renew and privacy protection at checkout; both are usually free.

Then, in the first week

  • Reserve the WordPress.org plugin slug. First come, permanent, and free. This is the one that cannot be undone later.
  • Take the social handles — the same name everywhere, even if you never post.
  • Set up a simple email address on the domain. It makes everything after this easier.

Then, before you spend anything on design

Get a trademark search done. A free domain and an empty Google result prove that nobody is using the name loudly. They cannot prove nobody has registered it quietly — those are different databases and only a lawyer can read them properly. Ask for a search in the software classes (9 and 42) covering India, the United States, the EU and the UK.

It is slow and it is cheap. Skipping it is fast and expensive — a competitor of ours has now renamed three times in five years, and its customers are publicly angry about it.

The one-line summary

Buy moorkeep.com this week. It is the only route where you own your name completely — the domain, the search results, and the trademark — for the entire life of the company, starting at about a thousand rupees a year.

If none of the three names feels right when you say it out loud, tell me and I will run another batch. The saying-it-out-loud test is the one that matters most, and it is the only one I cannot run for you.
